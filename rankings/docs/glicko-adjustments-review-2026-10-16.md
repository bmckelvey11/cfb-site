# Fix the preseason, then retune home field

Our Glicko-margin rating gets the core design right. Its opponent adjustment, its use of raw final margin, and its roughly 0.70 offseason carryover all match what the literature and the strongest public systems do. The adjustments it gets wrong, or skips, cluster in two places. The biggest is **the start of the season**: we already trail the Bovada opener by about 0.46 points of MAE, mostly in weeks 1–3. Our preseason step pulls every team the same 30% toward its conference mean and anchors conference switchers to the wrong conference. SP+, by contrast, makes the amount of regression depend on each team's roster continuity, including incoming transfers. The second is **home field**. Our flat 2.0 points sits below the best schedule-adjusted estimate for every season we rate: about 3.4 points in 2013, falling to about 2.4 by 2023. It also misses the 2020 empty-stadium dip. Everything else (travel, rest, venue-specific home field, garbage time, FCS weighting, weekly mean reversion) is either small, untested in college football, or both. Altitude and weather should not go into the margin model at all. One caveat runs through this whole report: the primary methodology pages for ESPN FPI, 538 and Sagarin could not be fetched. Several "what top systems do" entries are therefore secondhand or missing, and most effect sizes outside home field have no schedule-adjusted college estimate behind them.

The ranking rests on one tested fact about our own model: the market beats us most at the start of the season, so the adjustments that shape week-1 ratings come first. Home field ranks third, not first, because the bias is real but modest in the current era, and the fix is a one-parameter refit that can be done in an afternoon.

## Our preseason step treats every roster as equally stable

The strongest public system does not use a fixed regression. SP+ builds its preseason number from three parts. Last year's rating adjusted by returning production **"makes up about two-thirds of the projections formula"**. The rest is recruiting, weighted toward the latest class and including incoming transfers' recruiting ranks, plus "a sliver" of the previous four seasons ([Connelly via 6abc, 2025](https://6abc.com/post/spring-update-2025-college-football-sp-rankings-every-fbs-team/16504408/)). The practical difference from our model is that a team returning most of its starters regresses less than a team that lost them. Our model pulls everyone 30%. Massey takes a third approach: a weighted average of a team's own past final ratings, "damped out completely" as the season goes ([The Sports Arsenal quoting Massey](https://thesportsarsenal.com/2016/07/04/preseason-football-ratings-and-rankings-featuring-the-citadel-and-the-rest-of-the-socon/)). FPI's and 538's preseason weights could not be verified.

Our average amount of regression is not the problem. In the NFL, Glickman and Stern estimated a season-to-season carryover of **0.67** ([Glickman & Stern, glicko.net](http://www.glicko.net/research/nfl-chapter.pdf)), and Lopez, Matthews and Baumer found **0.69** ([arXiv 1701.05976](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.05976)). Our 0.70 sits right on top of those numbers. What is missing is team-to-team variation. Connelly's descriptive record shows how large it is: over the three seasons to 2025, teams with 70%+ returning production improved **70% of the time by an average of +7.1 SP+ points**, while teams under 40% regressed about 75% of the time, and **half fell by 10 or more points** ([Connelly via 6abc, 2025](https://6abc.com/post/college-football-2025-returning-production-136-fbs-teams/15951013/)). These are hit rates, not out-of-sample error figures. No public post-2021 test of returning production's predictive power was found. Still, swings of that size dwarf every situational term in this report.

That leaves the question of why our returning-production prior worked in 2014–19 and failed in 2021–26. The notes support three explanations, none yet confirmed on our data. First, the transfer portal: Connelly folds incoming transfers' production into both the numerator and the denominator of returning production, and gives FCS transfers half credit ([6abc, 2025](https://6abc.com/post/spring-update-2025-college-football-sp-rankings-every-fbs-team/16504408/)). CFBD's `percentPPA` appears to count returning players only. That would understate continuity for teams that reload through the portal. This is an inference to check against CFBD's documentation, not a verified fact. Second, the national average moved under our feet. Transfers rose from **13.9 to 22.8 per team in two years**, and average returning production slid "way past" its pre-COVID level of about 62% ([Connelly via abc7, 2026](https://abc7.com/post/what-returning-production-looks-like-2026-college-football-season/18754857/)). We do z-score per season, which handles a moving average, but not a change in what the stat means. Third, recruiting: Connelly says recruiting's weight in SP+ "has diminished significantly" for 2026 ([abc7, 2026](https://abc7.com/post/what-returning-production-looks-like-2026-college-football-season/18754857/)). That is consistent with our recruiting-heavy prior hurting after 2021. One small 2022–23 regression found recruiting still beat portal rankings at explaining end-of-season Elo. Its sample was two seasons, and its target was rating level, not change ([Beyond the Score](https://beyondthescoresports.substack.com/p/does-success-in-the-transfer-portal)).

The recommendation is to test a prior in which the offseason pull and the offseason variance both depend on roster continuity. That means less regression and less added variance for high-continuity teams, and more of both for teams with heavy turnover or portal intake. Build a transfer-inclusive returning-production measure, and cut the recruiting weight. Score it on 2021–26 walk-forward folds against the opening line, since weeks 1–3 are where we lose.

### Conference switchers are anchored to the wrong league

Regressing a team toward its **old** conference is backwards whenever the move reflects program quality, as with a Group of Five team moving up. No source documents how SP+, FPI, 538 or Sagarin handle switchers, so this rests on design logic, not evidence. Two alternatives avoid the problem: regress toward the new conference's mean, or toward the team's own rating history, which is Massey's approach. One public CFB Elo, ZeLO, regresses toward the conference mean and seeds teams that moved up from FCS with their FCS-era history instead of a generic start ([Zwiller](https://thomaszwiller.substack.com/p/ncaaf-elo-model)). The fix is a few lines of code and touches only switchers, so it should be tested together with the rank-1 prior work.

## Home field is worth more than 2.0 points in every season we rate

The best schedule-adjusted estimate comes from Benz, Bliss and Lopez's Bayesian model of 2004–2023. It puts FBS home field at **2.39 points in 2023 (95% interval 1.89–2.88)**, down from about **3.65 in 2008**, with a trend of **−0.097 points per season** and a posterior probability of decline of essentially 1 ([arXiv 2401.16392](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.16392)). Extending that slope back gives about 3.4 points in 2013. Against that, our flat 2.0 is about 1.4 points too low at the start of our window, and about 0.4 too low now. Practitioners agree on the current range. Makinen puts "true" CFB home field "closer to 2.6" and says books typically use about 3.0 ([VSiN](https://vsin.com/college-football/determining-college-football-true-home-field-advantage/)). Action Network starts from a 2.5 "standard" ([Action Network](https://www.actionnetwork.com/ncaaf/home-field-advantage-college-football-spread-betting)). VSiN's Burke uses a 2.0 default for 2026 ([VSiN 2026](https://vsin.com/college-football/college-football-home-field-advantage-rankings-for-2026/)), so 2.0 is not indefensible now. It is simply at the low end.

2020 runs the other way. Makinen says empty stadiums cut the traditional edge "roughly in half" ([VSiN](https://vsin.com/college-football/determining-college-football-true-home-field-advantage/)), and FBS home win rate through week 4 of 2020 was on pace for its lowest since 2005 ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/with-near-empty-stands-college-footballs-home-field-advantage-on-pace-to-be-worst-in-15-years)). No schedule-adjusted 2020 CFB estimate exists, and the academic paper simply dropped the season. In 2020 our 2.0 is probably too high.

Why this matters beyond the home games themselves: when the home edge is set too low, the model credits the leftover home-field points to team strength. Power-conference teams that buy home games against weak opponents get inflated, and the bias carries forward into neutral-site and road forecasts. The recommended test is to fit home field per season, or as a slow-moving state, from our own walk-forward data, with a 2020 indicator. One methodological warning: the estimate must hold team strength fixed. A random-team-effects model overestimated a true 3.0 home edge at **3.37**, because strong teams schedule home games against weak ones. Fixed team effects, or intraconference games only, removed the bias ([arXiv 1806.08059](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.08059)). Our Kalman ratings serve as the fixed team strengths, so fitting home field on our own innovations is the right approach. A raw "home teams win by X" average is not.

### Venue-specific home field is mostly noise

Handicapper tables put individual teams anywhere from **+9.5 (Jacksonville State) to −5.4 (Charlotte)** ([VSiN](https://vsin.com/college-football/determining-college-football-true-home-field-advantage/)). These are unshrunk estimates, and the evidence says they do not predict. In the NFL, past team home field has essentially zero out-of-sample predictive power (R² near 0) ([nfelo](https://www.nfeloapp.com/analysis/an-initial-exploration-of-home-field-advantage-in-the-nfl/)). In college basketball, KenPom calls his own team-level numbers "mostly for conversational fodder" ([KenPom](https://kenpom.com/blog/how-to-measure-site-specific-home-court-advantage-part-two/)). Massey estimates team-specific values but insists "a strong prior distribution must be used" ([Massey](https://masseyratings.com/theory/massey.htm)). No CFB out-of-sample test was found. Skip per-team values. If anything, test a coarse tier, such as Power vs. Group of Five, or conference vs. non-conference games, with heavy shrinkage.

### Some "neutral" games are home games

Our model gives zero home edge at every neutral site, so a team playing an hour from campus gets nothing. FPI's travel term (secondhand) gives such games partial home value automatically ([Wikipedia: FPI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Power_Index)). No study quantifies home edge at de facto home neutral sites. Test a simple proximity or in-state flag carrying a fitted fraction of home field. Separately, check that College Football Playoff games on campus (since 2024) are coded as true home games, not neutral.

## Score handling is mostly right already

Every established score-based predictor compresses margin, but smoothly. Sagarin's PREDICTOR applies "a law of diminishing returns," so 35–0 earns about the same as 70–0 ([Wikipedia: Sagarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Sagarin)). Massey maps each score to a 0–1 value through a normal curve that "flattens out for blowout scores" ([Massey FAQ](https://masseyratings.com/faq.php)). 538's Elo multiplies by log(margin + 1) and adds a correction for favorites ([Neil Paine](https://neilpaine.substack.com/p/nfl-elo-ratings-are-back)). That explains why our hard 24-point cap hurt. A hard cap throws away everything beyond 24 points. One NFL simulation puts the point where margin stops carrying information at **about 34 points** ([Football Perspective](https://www.footballperspective.com/whats-the-correct-margin-of-victory-cap-for-the-nfl/)), and CFB margins are more spread out than NFL margins. It also explains why the 2.5-SD clip did nothing: with a residual SD near 15, the clip only fires on surprises of about 38 points or more, which are rare.

Our Kalman form also does not need 538's favorite correction. That patch exists because Elo multiplies a win/loss update by margin, which systematically over-credits favorites. Our update already subtracts the expected margin, so the average surprise is zero by construction, as long as the forecast is unbiased. The way to check that is a diagnostic, not a new feature. Regress each game's surprise on its forecast margin, overall and separately for FBS-vs-FCS games. A slope away from zero means blowouts grow faster or slower than the model expects, and that ratings drift as a result.

If you want to try something beyond raw margin, the candidates are a Student-t likelihood (the update shrinks smoothly as the surprise grows), a signed-square-root or log transform applied to both observed and predicted margin, or a measurement variance that grows with the expected total. The literature does not crown any of them. A WPI thesis applied log, square-root and threshold transforms to the Glickman–Stern NFL model and reported no numeric gain in its abstract ([WPI](https://digitalcommons.wpi.edu/etd-theses/739)).

**Garbage time** is a separate, untested idea. CFBD flags plays after leads of 38/28/22 points in the second, third and fourth quarters ([CFBD](https://api.collegefootballdata.com/metrics-and-definitions.md)). Connelly uses similar cutoffs to filter SP+'s efficiency stats ([Football Study Hall](https://www.footballstudyhall.com/2017/10/20/16507348/college-football-analytics-game-states)). No public source tests whether a final-score rating does better using the margin at the moment garbage time begins. We could test it with CFBD play-by-play, and that result would be new evidence. The larger, costlier version of the same idea is a second observation stream, such as non-garbage EPA per play, fed into the filter with its own noise level. The likely reason SP+ and FPI work is that per-play efficiency is much less noisy than final score, not that they handle blowouts better. No public head-to-head comparison of score-based and efficiency-based CFB ratings was found ([The Power Rank](https://thepowerrank.com/guide-cfb-rankings/) reports that schedule-adjusted margin methods perform "almost as well as the closing spread," but gives no efficiency-system numbers).

**FCS opponents** belong in the pool. CFBD's SRS ([CFBD SRS](https://api.collegefootballdata.com/srs-ratings.md)), Massey and Sagarin all rate them. SP+ added conference-level adjustments for "connectivity issues" that made it "more accurate overall" but "hurt good mid-major teams' ratings a good amount" ([Football Study Hall](https://www.footballstudyhall.com/2019/4/11/18305971/2016-college-football-rankings)). The Kalman way to down-weight mismatches is a larger measurement variance for FBS-vs-FCS games, not a cap. Test that if the forecast-margin diagnostic above shows a slope in those games. Key numbers (9.6% of CFB games end at exactly 3 and 7.3% at exactly 7 ([arXiv 2212.08116](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08116))) matter when pricing cover and push probabilities, not in the rating update. Handle them at the betting layer.

## The two sigmas hint at something misspecified

The calibration fix worked: 68% intervals now cover 68.9%, not 72.3%. The numbers also line up with the market. With σ_pred = 13.5 and team uncertainties of about 3–5 points each, total forecast SD comes to about 14.1–15.2. That matches the published CFB spread-residual range of **14 to 15.4** ([arXiv 2212.08116](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08116); [The Only Colors](https://www.theonlycolors.com/2020/9/29/21492301/vegas-always-knows-a-mathematical-deep-dive)), and the CFB market's best-fit conversion of **14.112** ([The Only Colors](https://www.theonlycolors.com/2020/9/29/21492301/vegas-always-knows-a-mathematical-deep-dive)). Keep it.

The theory says one number should serve both jobs, though. In a correct state-space model, forecast variance equals game noise plus the two teams' rating variances, so the same game σ appears in the update and in the forecast ([Lopez et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.05976)). No source was found that recommends separating them. Needing 15 for the update and 13.5 for the forecast points to one of a few problems. Team variances may run too high, from too much weekly or offseason process noise. The 15 may be quietly acting as outlier protection. Or the noise may differ by game type and get averaged into one number. The test is to fit game σ, weekly process variance and a weekly carryover together by maximizing one-step-ahead predictive likelihood, the Glickman–Stern approach. If the fitted game σ lands near 13.5–14 and the update still prefers 15, the 15 is a regularizer, and a Student-t likelihood is the principled replacement. On variance that depends on the spread, the only public test found a null result: correlation 0.09 between spread size and ATS-residual SD in CFB ([Boyd's Bets](https://www.boydsbets.com/ats-margin-standard-deviations-by-point-spread/)). Variance by total, by week, and in FCS games is untested anywhere, so our own data would have to answer those questions.

### Week-to-week drift probably needs a small pull back

Our in-season model is a pure random walk. NFL state-space fits find a weekly carryover of **0.978**, not 1.0 ([Lopez et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.05976)), which compounds over about 17 weeks into roughly as much shrinkage as an offseason. MLB came out at 1.002 ("quite possibly follow a random walk"), so the answer varies by sport, and no CFB estimate exists. This belongs inside the same joint fit as the sigmas. Test a weekly carryover just below 1, pulling toward the preseason prior, and let the data choose it.

## Situational terms are small, and some are traps

**Travel and time zones** rank first among the situational terms, though still far below the items above. FPI reportedly counts a Seattle-to-Miami trip as "about a half point per game" ([Wikipedia: FPI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Power_Index), secondhand). Coleman's peer-reviewed study found the CFB betting market an "inaccurate and inefficient" processor of travel effects. The most consistent edge was late-season underdogs facing an opponent with a one-hour time-zone advantage, though the size is behind a paywall ([UNF Scholars](https://scholars.unf.edu/en/publications/team-travel-effects-and-the-college-football-betting-market/)). Studies that report 5–7 point travel penalties do not adjust for team strength, so ignore those sizes ([UTS OPUS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/128688)). Test a signed time-zone differential and a distance term against closing lines.

**Rest and byes** have only thin college evidence. One 2015 sample of 81 games showed teams off a bye at +2.3 points against the spread, and its author calls it "a one-year glimpse" ([Football Study Hall](https://www.footballstudyhall.com/2016/2/2/10889450/college-football-bye-weeks-do-they-help)). The NFL bye edge was **+2.2 before 2011 and about +0.3 after**, when the league banned practice during byes ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frbhe.2024.1479832/full)). College teams still practice during byes, so the pre-2011 NFL figure is the closer analog, but that is reasoning, not measurement. Test it, at low priority.

**QB changes and injuries** are the biggest honest unknown. No sourced CFB point value for a starting quarterback was found. SP+ handles QBs only through the 22% weight on QB passing in offensive returning production ([abc7, 2026](https://abc7.com/post/what-returning-production-looks-like-2026-college-football-season/18754857/)), and FPI's in-season injury handling could not be verified. A preseason version can piggyback on the transfer-inclusive returning-production work in rank 1. An in-season injury term needs a timely starter feed, and that is a data problem before it is a modeling one.

**Altitude: skip.** FPI reportedly found it "not significant for college football" ([Wikipedia: FPI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Power_Index)), and high-elevation FBS stadiums show no significant home-win difference ([JNSSM](https://jnssm.uk.ac.ir/article_4202.html)). Any Mountain West edge is better captured by travel and time-zone terms. **Weather: skip for margins.** The evidence found concerns totals: bad weather lowers scoring, and the NFL totals market under-reacts ([RePEc, Borghesi 2008](https://ideas.repec.org:443/a/taf/apfiec/v18y2008i12p947-953.html)). No CFB study shows weather shifting the expected margin. If weather ever enters, it belongs in the variance, and only with forecasts available before kickoff, never observed weather.

## Conclusion

The model's remaining gap to the market is mainly a problem of knowing too little at the start of the season, not one of missing adjustments. The situational terms that pundits like to argue about (altitude, byes, venue-specific home field) are either unmeasurable or worth fractions of a point. Roster turnover in the portal era moves teams by touchdowns. The important question is whether a continuity-aware, transfer-inclusive prior can shrink our weeks 1–3 error where the recruiting-heavy prior failed. The cheap home-field refit and the conference-switcher fix should ride along in the same round of testing.

The two-sigma question is worth more than it looks. If one joint likelihood fit of game noise, weekly process variance and weekly carryover lets a single σ do both jobs, it also settles the random-walk question and tells us how much to trust early-season ratings. That is the same weeks 1–3 problem from the other side. Every "test" above should be scored the way the repository's evaluation standard requires: walk-forward folds, an interval rather than a point estimate, and comparison against the same-time de-vigged market. Most of the effect sizes in this report have no college-football out-of-sample evidence behind them, so our own backtests will be the first real verdict on them.

## Added after a code check (2026-10-16)

- **Schedule strength never gets credited after the fact.** SP+, FPI, Sagarin and Massey re-fit the whole season, so a win over a team that later proves good gains value. `GlickoMargin.update` moves only the two teams in the game and keeps each team's variance separate, so what is learned about an opponent later never flows back to the teams it played. A joint season fit, or a filter that tracks covariance between teams, would fix it. Expected to matter most early in the season and through the sparse FBS–FCS links; untested.
- **CFBD's returning production ignores incoming transfers**, and its national average swung from 76.7% returning in 2021 to 53.7% in 2025. Together these explain why the P2 prior broke ([`glicko-p2-2026-10-15.md`](glicko-p2-2026-10-15.md)).
- **Smaller items:** score overtime games as a tie at the end of regulation, and weight bowl games and FBS-vs-FCS games less.
